How to build a marketing function before you can afford marketers

To build a marketing function in a small business before you can hire, set the decisions first (who you sell to, what they should believe, which one channel you own), then build AI-powered systems that produce against those decisions every week. A fractional CMO can set both up and hand them over, so your first marketing hire inherits a running system instead of a blank page.

Build a marketing function in a small business: start with decisions, not hires

Building a marketing function in a small business starts with five decisions, not five job descriptions. Who is the ideal customer. What should they believe about you that they don't believe today. Which single channel you will own for the next six months. What a qualified lead looks like. How you will know, each month, whether any of it worked. A marketing function is those decisions plus a weekly rhythm that turns them into output. People are one way to supply the output. They are not the function.

That distinction matters because of money. Gartner's 2024 CMO Spend Survey found marketing budgets fell to 7.7% of company revenue, down from 9.1% in 2023. If large companies with established marketing departments are cutting, a twenty-person business with no marketing department is not about to fund a team of four. The founder ends up doing marketing at 11pm, in between everything else, with no decisions written down. Every post starts from zero.

Cheaper people won't fix that. Make the decisions once, write them down, and build the production so it doesn't depend on anyone's spare evening.

Four ways to staff it before you can hire

Most small businesses pick one of four set-ups before the first full-time marketing hire. They differ less on cost than on one question: what is still standing when the person doing the work leaves?

The junior hire deserves a closer look, because it's the most common choice and the most expensive mistake. A first marketer at two or three years of experience gets handed the function and asked to invent the strategy, run the channels and report to a founder who has no time to coach. Six months later the calendar is full and the pipeline is the same. The person isn't the problem. The seat was designed for someone with fifteen years behind them.

What the AI layer actually does, and what it can't

An AI marketing system is a set of connected workflows that research, draft, personalise and report on a schedule, with a human approving what goes out. For a small business, four of them cover most of the production a two- or three-person team would otherwise handle.

A research agent watches your market: competitor pages, buyer questions in forums, news about the accounts you want. It delivers a short brief every week so content and outreach start from something real. A content engine turns one founder conversation or one customer call into a blog article, three LinkedIn posts and a newsletter section, in your voice, against your positioning. The step-by-step build of an AI content engine covers how that pipeline is wired. An outreach drafter reads a target account and writes a first message the founder edits and sends. A reporting loop pulls the month's numbers from GA4, the CRM and LinkedIn into one page with three questions on it.

Adoption isn't the barrier anymore. McKinsey's State of AI survey, published in May 2024, found 65% of organizations were regularly using generative AI, nearly double the share ten months earlier, with marketing and sales the function where use was most common. The barrier is direction. An AI system with no positioning behind it produces more of the generic content your buyers already scroll past, faster.

What AI can't do: pick your market, decide what you stand for, or tell you to stop doing the thing that isn't working. Those are judgement calls, and they're exactly the calls a small business can't make well alone because nobody inside has done it ten times before. AI in the engine. Strategy in front.

A build order that works in five steps

1. Write the decisions down

Ideal customer, positioning, the one belief you want to change, and what counts as a qualified lead. One page. If it doesn't fit on one page, it isn't decided yet. Every system downstream reads from this document, so it's the most valuable hour you'll spend. (Yes, founders always want to skip to the tools. The tools are the easy part.)

2. Pick one channel and own it

LinkedIn, search, outbound, partnerships, events. Pick the one where your buyers already spend attention and commit for two quarters. Spreading a small budget across five channels buys you presence on five channels and traction on none.

3. Build the systems around that channel

Research, content, outreach and reporting, each configured for the chosen channel and the written positioning. This is where a fractional CMO who builds earns the fee: the strategy and the plumbing come from the same head, so nothing gets lost in a handover between a strategist and a technician.

4. Hand over with runbooks and a named owner

Every system gets a one-page runbook: what it does, when it runs, what to check, what to do when it breaks. Every system gets an owner inside the business, usually the founder or an operations lead, with one to two hours of review a week. Running before I leave is the standard.

5. Review monthly, change one thing at a time

Once a month, look at the reporting page and change one variable: a topic, a target segment, a message. Change five things at once and you'll learn nothing about which one mattered.

Honest caveat: systems decay. Models change, APIs change, a LinkedIn update breaks a workflow on a Tuesday. A system nobody owns is running on borrowed time, and no amount of good architecture fixes an empty owner seat. That's the real cost of this model, and it's smaller than a salary, but it isn't zero.

When to hire your first full-time marketer

Hire when three things are true at once. The systems produce more than the founder can review each week. One channel has proven it brings qualified pipeline, not just impressions. And the work now needs someone inside the business every day, at the customer calls and the product meetings. Before that, a full-time senior hire is paying to discover what a fractional set-up could have discovered for a fraction of the commitment.

The numbers make the case plainly. A senior marketing leader at a scale-up costs roughly €120,000 to €150,000 a year fully loaded. In France, a permanent contract costs about 1.45 times gross salary once employer contributions are in, and about 1.55 times with paid leave and absences counted. A fractional CMO is paid only for the days worked, and the fee depends on rhythm and scope, agreed at the first call.

The real gain shows up on day one of the first hire. They don't arrive to a blank page and a founder saying "do marketing." They arrive to a strategy document, four running systems, a year of reporting and a channel that works. Their job is to scale it. You can't afford a marketing team yet. You can afford the decisions a marketing team would run on, and the systems that keep running once they're made. Build the system once. Let it run.

Frequently asked questions

How do you build a marketing function in a small business with no marketing team?

Start with the decisions, not the hires. Write down who you sell to, what you want them to believe, and which single channel you will own. Then build the production around that channel: a research routine, a content engine, an outreach drafting flow and a monthly report. A fractional CMO can set the decisions and build those systems in a few weeks, and the founder keeps them running with an hour or two of review a week.

Can AI replace a marketing team in a small business?

AI can replace a large share of marketing production: research, first drafts, repurposing, outreach personalisation, reporting. It cannot replace the judgement that sits above production: choosing the market, setting the positioning, deciding what to stop. A small business that automates production without those decisions gets more output pointed in no particular direction.

What does a fractional CMO do when building a marketing function?

A fractional CMO sets the strategy (ideal customer, positioning, the one channel worth owning), builds the AI-powered systems that produce against that strategy, documents how each system runs, and hands them to an owner inside the business. The goal is a function that keeps running after the engagement ends, and a first hire who inherits a working system instead of a blank page.

When should a small business hire its first full-time marketer?

Hire when the systems produce more than the founder can review, when one channel has proven it brings qualified pipeline, and when the work needs someone inside the business every day. A full-time senior marketing leader at a scale-up costs roughly 120,000 to 150,000 euros a year fully loaded, so the hire should scale something that already works, not discover what might.
